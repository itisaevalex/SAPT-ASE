# Machine Learning Models for SAPT0 Energy Component Prediction

## Introduction

Predicting **symmetry-adapted perturbation theory (SAPT0)** energy components directly from molecular geometry is a challenging task with high practical value. SAPT0 provides a physically interpretable breakdown of noncovalent interaction energy into **electrostatics**, **exchange-repulsion**, **induction (polarization)**, **dispersion**, and the **total** interaction energy. Accurate ML models can serve as surrogates for these costly quantum calculations, enabling rapid screening of molecular complexes with near-ab initio accuracy. We focus on state-of-the-art architectures that take **3D geometries as input** – notably graph neural networks (GNNs) and SE(3)-**equivariant neural networks** – which inherently respect molecular symmetries. Descriptor-based models are covered only briefly, and only when they achieve exceptional performance or efficiency.

## Model Architectures for SAPT0 Component Prediction

### Graph Neural Networks and Equivariant Models

**Graph neural networks (GNNs)** process molecules as graphs of atoms and bonds, naturally handling 3D geometry via interatomic distances or coordinate tensors. Modern GNN variants can be made **rotation- and translation-equivariant**, ensuring predictions that do not depend on arbitrary orientation of the input. A prime example is **AP-Net** (Atomic Pairwise Neural Network), an architecture specifically designed to predict all four SAPT0 components (Eelst, Eexch, Eind, Edisp) as well as the total interaction energy. AP-Net consists of two stages: an **atomic property module** that assigns each atom learned multipole moments up to quadrupoles (enforcing physical constraints like rotational equivariance and charge conservation), and an **interaction energy module** that takes two monomers’ atom-wise features (including the learned multipoles) to predict pairwise interaction contributions. By summing atomic pair contributions, AP-Net yields the full SAPT0 energy decomposition. Notably, AP-Net incorporates a **multipolar electrostatics baseline** – computing the long-range Eelst from predicted multipoles – and trains the network to predict the remaining short-range contributions as a correction. This physics-informed design improves accuracy and transferability, as it cleanly separates long-range Coulomb interactions, which are well-captured by classical multipole models, from the harder-to-model short-range quantum terms.

Another category of cutting-edge models are **SE(3)-equivariant transformers**, such as those implemented in TorchMD-NET. These models use attention mechanisms on sets of atoms and encode geometric relations via tensor algebra, maintaining equivariance to rotations and translations. An _equivariant transformer (ET)_ processes 3D coordinates and atom types to learn complex interaction patterns; it has achieved state-of-the-art performance on general quantum chemistry tasks (MD17 molecular forces, QM9 properties, etc.). While not yet widely applied specifically to SAPT0 components, recent work indicates their potential: for example, an ET from TorchMD-NET was successfully used to predict accurate **molecular cluster interaction energies**, achieving high accuracy on lattice energy benchmarks (comparable to or better than traditional methods that use SAPT). The **E(3)-equivariant** design of such transformers means they could predict a full SAPT decomposition if trained on those targets, combining the expressiveness of attention networks with built-in physical symmetry constraints. In principle, any message-passing neural network or transformer that operates on atomic coordinates can be extended to multi-output predictions (one output per SAPT component plus total). Indeed, AP-Net’s interaction module can be viewed as a specialized message-passing network producing multiple energy components. Equivariant transformers and GNNs offer a general and flexible framework for directly learning from geometries **without hand-crafted descriptors**, a key advantage as it avoids losing information and can adapt to diverse chemical environments.

**Notable GNN/GNN-like Models:** _AP-Net_ is currently the flagship in this category, as it was explicitly designed for SAPT0 predictions. It demonstrated that an equivariant MPNN with a physically motivated architecture can achieve high fidelity for all components. Another example is the recent equivariant GNN by Thürlemann _et al._ (2022) which, while aimed at force field parametrization, showed how including SAPT0 component terms in the loss function can regularize training ([](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/bitstream/handle/20.500.11850/550373/1/2206.00355.pdf#:~:text=theory%20%28SAPT%29%20,terms%20in%20the%20fitting%20procedure)). That model used a GNN to learn parameters of a fixed functional form and included SAPT0-derived targets (dispersion, electrostatics, etc.) during fitting ([](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/bitstream/handle/20.500.11850/550373/1/2206.00355.pdf#:~:text=theory%20%28SAPT%29%20,terms%20in%20the%20fitting%20procedure)), effectively blending ML flexibility with a SAPT-guided physics basis. This highlights a trend: even in primarily physics-based models, GNNs can be used to predict or refine the energy contributions associated with each physical term.

**Equivariance and Transferability:** A major benefit of these geometry-based models is their good transferability across chemical space. Because they learn from coordinates and fundamental atomic properties, they can generalize to new molecules or configurations better than many descriptor-bound models. AP-Net, for instance, after training on a large set of dimers, was able to predict interaction energies for **chemically diverse systems (S66x8 set)** with high accuracy, even for interaction types (like π–π stacking) not emphasized in its training data. The learned atomic representations allowed it to extrapolate beyond hydrogen-bonded systems and still produce reasonable predictions for dispersion-dominated cases like stacked aromatics. This suggests that well-designed equivariant GNNs capture underlying physics that remains valid outside the immediate training distribution.

### Descriptor-Based and Physics-Informed Models

In contrast to end-to-end geometry learners, **descriptor-based models** rely on engineered features or functional forms as an intermediate representation. These methods have a longer history in force-field development, and a few have achieved remarkable accuracy by combining physical knowledge with machine learning.

A prominent example is **CLIFF** – the _Component-Based, Machine-Learned Intermolecular Force Field_ introduced by Schriber _et al._ ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=we%20introduce%20the%20component,The%20CLIFF%20achieves)). CLIFF explicitly mirrors the SAPT decomposition: it uses separate functional terms corresponding to electrostatics, exchange-repulsion, induction, and dispersion, just as in SAPT ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=we%20introduce%20the%20component,The%20CLIFF%20achieves)) ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=interaction%20energies%20with%20machine,side)). The functional forms are physics-based (e.g. Coulombic terms for electrostatics, exponentially decaying repulsion terms, dispersion damped-attraction terms, etc.), but their parameters are not fixed. Instead, CLIFF employs machine learning (specifically, kernel ridge regression models with carefully chosen descriptors) to predict the **molecule-specific parameters** for each component ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=electrostatic%2C%20exchange,protein%20fragments%2C%20the%20CLIFF%20produces)) ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=SAPT2%2B%283%29%CE%B4MP2%2Faug,the%20CLIFF%20is%20able%20to)). For instance, atomic partial charges, multipole moments, effective atomic sizes (“widths”), and dispersion coefficients are determined via ML models trained on a library of reference dimers. The training data for CLIFF consisted of ~10k diverse dimer interactions computed at a high level (SAPT2+3δMP2/aug-cc-pVTZ, which is beyond SAPT0) to ensure **near gold-standard accuracy** ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=electrostatic%2C%20exchange,protein%20fragments%2C%20the%20CLIFF%20produces)) ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=learning%20models%20developed%20for%20C%2C,error)). By fitting to this higher-fidelity data, CLIFF effectively learns a transferable force field that reproduces SAPT components at a more accurate level than SAPT0 itself. Notably, CLIFF achieved **mean absolute errors (MAEs) below 0.70 kcal/mol for each energy component and the total energy** across a broad test set of dimers ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=electrostatic%2C%20exchange,protein%20fragments%2C%20the%20CLIFF%20produces)). On a specialized side-chain interaction benchmark (protein fragment dimers akin to a SAPT-PDB set), it reached an even lower total MAE of 0.27 kcal/mol ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=SAPT2%2B%283%29%CE%B4MP2%2Faug,the%20CLIFF%20is%20able%20to)) ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=learning%20models%20developed%20for%20C%2C,error)) – an exceptionally high accuracy indicating nearly all predictions were within chemical accuracy (~1 kcal/mol). The CLIFF approach is **fast** and **lightweight** at inference: since it reduces the problem to computing a known functional form with ML-fitted parameters, evaluating a new dimer’s energy just involves computing the chosen descriptors (handled by an efficient library) and a few matrix operations from the KRR model, followed by summing the analytic energy terms. This typically takes only seconds on a single CPU for a complex of moderate size, comparable to a classical force field evaluation. Indeed, the CLIFF code is available as a Python package ([GitHub - jeffschriber/cliff: Component-based Learned Intermolecular Force Field](https://github.com/jeffschriber/cliff#:~:text=Running%20the%20Code)) ([GitHub - jeffschriber/cliff: Component-based Learned Intermolecular Force Field](https://github.com/jeffschriber/cliff#:~:text=CLIFF%20can%20be%20run%20using,written%20python%20scripts)), leveraging the QML library for descriptor computations (e.g. FCHL or symmetry functions) and running efficiently on standard hardware (no GPU is required) ([GitHub - jeffschriber/cliff: Component-based Learned Intermolecular Force Field](https://github.com/jeffschriber/cliff#:~:text=For%20now%2C%20we%20use%20the,install%20QML%20is%20with%20pip)) ([GitHub - jeffschriber/cliff: Component-based Learned Intermolecular Force Field](https://github.com/jeffschriber/cliff#:~:text=cliff%2Fmodels%2Flarge%20need%20to%20be%20un)). Integration into Python workflows is straightforward: users can call a provided CLIFF script or import it as a library to predict SAPT-like decomposed energies for any input dimer geometry ([GitHub - jeffschriber/cliff: Component-based Learned Intermolecular Force Field](https://github.com/jeffschriber/cliff#:~:text=Running%20the%20Code)) ([GitHub - jeffschriber/cliff: Component-based Learned Intermolecular Force Field](https://github.com/jeffschriber/cliff#:~:text=usage%3A%20run_cliff.py%20%5B,based%20Learned%20Intermolecular%20Force%20Field)).

Another descriptor-based approach is the earlier neural network model often referred to as **SAPT-ML** (a precursor to AP-Net’s development). SAPT-ML used hand-crafted intermolecular descriptors – specifically, **atomic environment symmetry functions (ACSFs)** – to characterize the dimer configuration ([](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/api-gateway/chemrxiv/assets/orp/resource/item/60c74aba842e65651edb2fcd/original/ap-net-an-atomic-pairwise-neural-network-for-smooth-and-transferable-interaction-potentials.pdf#:~:text=significant%20improvement%20over%20the%20intermolecular,Net%20and)). A feed-forward neural network (or committee of networks) was trained on SAPT0 data to predict the energy components. While SAPT-ML did succeed in learning the SAPT0 decomposition for hydrogen-bonded dimers, its accuracy and transferability were limited. For example, on a test set of N-methylacetamide (NMA) interacting with various donor/acceptor molecules, SAPT-ML’s total energy MAE was higher and its component-wise errors were consistently larger compared to AP-Net. AP-Net’s authors report that for 8 out of 10 diverse dimers in that test, AP-Net had lower error in every SAPT component and total energy, often roughly halving the MAE relative to SAPT-ML. One specific comparison noted that SAPT-ML’s predictions, while decent, sometimes benefited from fortuitous error cancellation and did not capture the correct asymptotic shape of the interaction potential in difficult cases (e.g. at longer-range separations) ([](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/api-gateway/chemrxiv/assets/orp/resource/item/60c74aba842e65651edb2fcd/original/ap-net-an-atomic-pairwise-neural-network-for-smooth-and-transferable-interaction-potentials.pdf#:~:text=significant%20improvement%20over%20the%20intermolecular,Net%20and)). The improved performance of AP-Net is attributed to its explicit physics-motivated design (learning multipoles and being rigorously equivariant) versus SAPT-ML’s reliance on fixed geometric descriptors. This underscores that while descriptor-based models can achieve _high accuracy within the training domain_, their lack of built-in physical constraints can hinder generalization. Nonetheless, SAPT-ML was an important step demonstrating that **all four SAPT0 components could be learned simultaneously** by an ML model – a proof-of-concept that informed later GNN approaches.

In summary, descriptor-based models like CLIFF (and to an extent SAPT-ML) incorporate substantial domain knowledge (via functional forms or features). They tend to be **efficient** at inference and can excel with limited training data by exploiting known physics. However, they may require more human effort to design and might be less adaptable to arbitrary new chemistry if it falls outside the span of their descriptors. On the other hand, graph/equivariant models automatically learn representations from raw geometry, offering more flexibility and, with sufficient data, excellent accuracy and transferability.

**Accuracy on S22/S66:** The S22 and S66 sets (small organic dimers) are relatively easy targets for these ML models. All the listed models, when appropriately trained, achieve mean errors on the order of a few tenths of a kcal/mol for such simple dimers. For instance, AP-Net and CLIFF were evaluated on subsets of S66x8 (which extends S66 by including stretched/compressed geometries): AP-Net produced physically correct potential energy curves and MAEs around 0.3–0.5 kcal/mol, outperforming a broad-purpose model like ANI-1x on these intermolecular cases. CLIFF’s reported errors (≤0.7 kcal/mol for worst-case components) were benchmarked on a diverse set that included S66 dimers ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=electrostatic%2C%20exchange,protein%20fragments%2C%20the%20CLIFF%20produces)), implying its performance on S22/S66 specifically is likely around or below 0.5 kcal/mol MAE (since those datasets are well-represented in the training). In short, both AP-Net and CLIFF comfortably reach “chemical accuracy” (~1 kcal/mol) for the standard small dimer benchmarks, with AP-Net often being a bit more accurate on average (benefiting from extensive training data ([](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/api-gateway/chemrxiv/assets/orp/resource/item/65ccd41866c1381729a2b885/original/a-physics-aware-neural-network-for-protein-ligand-interactions-with-quantum-chemical-accuracy.pdf#:~:text=over%2097,even%20greater%20accuracy%20than%20the)) and an expressive neural architecture) and CLIFF being very reliable due to its physics-grounded form ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=SAPT2%2B%283%29%CE%B4MP2%2Faug,the%20CLIFF%20is%20able%20to)).

**Performance on Large Datasets (SAPT-PDB-13K & Splinter):** The **SAPT-PDB-13K** set (13,216 protein–ligand fragment dimers) and **Splinter** (≈1.7 million generated dimers) pose a greater challenge due to their size and diversity. AP-Net was trained on the bulk of Splinter and validated on a 150k holdout, achieving ~0.2 kcal/mol MAE (total energy) and even smaller errors for individual components (e.g. ~0.1 kcal/mol for induction, ~0.02 for dispersion). This indicates that, given sufficient training coverage, the model can learn each component to high precision – dispersion tends to be easiest (small magnitude, smooth), while induction and electrostatics are somewhat harder. When tested on SAPT-PDB-13K (which AP-Net had not seen during training), the model’s accuracy naturally dips but remains good: around 0.74 kcal/mol MAE in total energy, with component-wise MAEs of 0.47 (Eelst), 0.18 (Eexch), 0.44 (Eind), and 0.06 (Edisp</sub). Most of those ~13k complexes are considerably larger and more heterogeneous than typical dimers, so an error under 1 kcal/mol is a strong result – indeed, **97%** of AP-Net’s predictions for the validation dimers are within 1 kcal/mol of SAPT0 values ([](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/api-gateway/chemrxiv/assets/orp/resource/item/65ccd41866c1381729a2b885/original/a-physics-aware-neural-network-for-protein-ligand-interactions-with-quantum-chemical-accuracy.pdf#:~:text=over%2097,even%20greater%20accuracy%20than%20the)), giving confidence for screening use. CLIFF’s approach has not been published on Splinter-scale data (and its kernel models might not scale to millions of examples without sparsification), but it excelled on a protein-fragment test set with an **MAE of 0.27 kcal/mol** ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=SAPT2%2B%283%29%CE%B4MP2%2Faug,the%20CLIFF%20is%20able%20to)). This likely reflects that the side-chain dataset (and similar protein fragment interactions) were within the scope of CLIFF’s training; it suggests that CLIFF can generalize well to biochemically relevant fragments and might maintain <1 kcal/mol accuracy for protein–ligand interactions as long as the functional groups involved were present in training. In practical terms, AP-Net currently has the edge in handling _ultra-large_ datasets (thanks to the scalability of deep learning with GPU acceleration and very large training sets like Splinter), whereas CLIFF shows that with a cleverly chosen ~10k training set, one can already cover a wide chemical space with near-DFT accuracy for components.

**Transferability Between Datasets:** A key question for multi-fidelity workflows is whether a model trained on one dataset can be applied or adapted to another. We saw that AP-Net trained on Splinter generalized to SAPT-PDB fragments reasonably well; additionally, earlier AP-Net versions trained on small-molecule dimers (NMA-based set) could predict S66x8 and even some experimental dimer geometries with decent accuracy ([](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/api-gateway/chemrxiv/assets/orp/resource/item/60c74aba842e65651edb2fcd/original/ap-net-an-atomic-pairwise-neural-network-for-smooth-and-transferable-interaction-potentials.pdf#:~:text=AP,37)). CLIFF, by virtue of its physics-based form, is inherently quite transferable – it can be deployed on new molecules without retraining, since it learns element-specific parameter functions. Its success in ranking larger protein–ligand complexes (outside its training set) within ~10% error of true SAPT indicates a high degree of transferability to novel systems ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=learning%20models%20developed%20for%20C%2C,error)) ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=component%20energies%20across%20a%20diverse,reference%20values%20for%20most%20complexes)). On the other hand, a purely descriptor-based NN like SAPT-ML showed limited transferability; it struggled if the new dimers involved interactions or chemical groups not well represented in training (this is evident from its higher errors on certain test dimers where AP-Net did better by leveraging physics). The bottom line is that **incorporating physical inductive biases (equivariance, functional decomposition)** greatly aids transfer: AP-Net and CLIFF can be taken as complementary demonstrations of this principle from the ML and the physics side, respectively.

## Inference Efficiency and Hardware Considerations

For deployment in screening and simulation workflows, **speed and memory footprint** are critical. All the discussed models offer **orders-of-magnitude speedups** over direct SAPT0 calculations. SAPT0 for a moderate-size dimer (tens to a couple hundred atoms) often takes hours on multi-core CPUs, whereas these ML models return results in seconds or less. AP-Net, for example, was benchmarked on protein–ligand complexes (~200 atoms) where SAPT0 took ~1.5 days on 12 CPU cores; AP-Net produced the same SAPT0-like output in only a few seconds per complex. This makes high-throughput _in silico_ screening feasible “with quantum-mechanical accuracy” in scenarios that were previously impractical. Similarly, CLIFF’s authors note that their model can be used routinely for drug-design contexts where evaluating new poses or analogues must be near-instantaneous ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=component%20energies%20across%20a%20diverse,reference%20values%20for%20most%20complexes)). In terms of **scaling with system size**, AP-Net’s atomic-pairwise summation means inference cost scales roughly with O(N2) for N atoms in the dimer. In practice, AP-Net was run on dimers up to ~300 atoms (the Splinter set includes fragments of that size) on a single GPU without issue. Its memory footprint is modest – the model has a fixed number of parameters (on the order of a few hundred thousand) and stores per-atom and per-pair intermediate tensors. A modern consumer GPU (with say 8–16 GB memory) or even a CPU with a few GB RAM is sufficient for typical cases. The AP-Net code can use batch processing to handle many dimers in parallel if GPU memory permits, further boosting throughput. **Equivariant transformers** like TorchMD-NET ET are heavier; attention layers scale as O(N^2) in computation and memory, so extremely large complexes (hundreds of atoms) may require more memory or sparse approximations. Nonetheless, TorchMD-NET has been optimized and can handle reasonably large structures; it was demonstrated on protein fragments and showed good scaling with model parallelism. For most “screening” scenarios (dimers or small complexes), an ET model would still run in a few seconds on a single GPU, which is acceptable.

Descriptor models like CLIFF are extremely lightweight: once the kernel models are loaded (~hundreds of KB to a few MB of data for the regression coefficients), predicting a new case mostly involves computing descriptors for each atom/fragment. The QML package used by CLIFF computes these (e.g. FCHL19 or Coulomb matrix representations) efficiently in C++, and the subsequent energy evaluation is a simple equation evaluation. This can be done on CPU with no significant lag – even without a GPU, CLIFF can evaluate thousands of dimers per hour on a normal laptop. Memory is rarely a bottleneck; CLIFF’s descriptors and parameters for eight element types easily fit in memory (and can be computed on the fly).

In terms of **hardware compatibility**, all models are usable on standard hardware. AP-Net training was done on GPUs, but inference can be done on CPU if needed (albeit slower). CLIFF was designed with CPU usage in mind (important for integration into existing simulation pipelines that may not use GPUs). TorchMD and other deep models typically assume a GPU for efficient training, but inference on CPU is possible for smaller molecules. **Consumer GPUs** (like an NVIDIA GeForce series with CUDA support) are more than sufficient to deploy these models; one does not require specialized AI hardware. For example, a pre-trained AP-Net model can be loaded and used via a Python script on any machine with TensorFlow – performing a single prediction involves a forward pass that takes negligible GPU time (and a fraction of a second on CPU for small systems). The largest memory demand might come from handling **batch uncertainty estimation** (e.g., running an ensemble of 5 models or doing multiple Monte Carlo dropout passes), but even that is manageable with sequential processing if memory is limited.

## Uncertainty Quantification and Reliability

In applied multi-fidelity settings, being able to **quantify uncertainty** in ML predictions is as important as raw accuracy. A model that can flag when a prediction is unreliable allows for a hybrid strategy: use the fast ML predictor most of the time, but fall back to a higher-fidelity (but slower) method when needed. Several approaches have been explored or proposed for uncertainty quantification (UQ) in this context:

- **Ensemble Models:** The simplest robust method is to train an ensemble (committee) of models and examine the variance in their predictions. AP-Net’s developers adopted an _ensemble averaging_ approach to improve reliability – they train multiple instances of the network (with different random initializations or data splits) and then take the average SAPT0 components as the prediction ([](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/api-gateway/chemrxiv/assets/orp/resource/item/65ccd41866c1381729a2b885/original/a-physics-aware-neural-network-for-protein-ligand-interactions-with-quantum-chemical-accuracy.pdf#:~:text=on%20the%20same%20data%20to,prepared%20to%20con%02sist%20of%20diverse)). The spread of the ensemble can serve as an uncertainty estimate. In practice, AP-Net’s reported errors (e.g. 0.20 kcal/mol MAE on validation) already include the benefit of model averaging, which tends to reduce random error. If one sets a threshold such as **σ > 0.3 kcal/mol** (where σ is the standard deviation of the ensemble’s predictions for a given dimer), very few normal cases would exceed this – those that do are likely outliers or novel chemistries where the model members disagree. Such a thresholding scheme could effectively flag <5% of cases for “manual” review by higher-level QM. For instance, in the Splinter validation set, an overwhelming majority (>97%) of dimers are predicted within 1 kcal/mol error ([](https://chemrxiv.org/engage/api-gateway/chemrxiv/assets/orp/resource/item/65ccd41866c1381729a2b885/original/a-physics-aware-neural-network-for-protein-ligand-interactions-with-quantum-chemical-accuracy.pdf#:~:text=over%2097,even%20greater%20accuracy%20than%20the)); the remaining few percent with larger errors could potentially be identified by an uncertainty metric. While AP-Net’s current publications don’t explicitly give a σ distribution, the principle is well-known: **deep ensembles** provide a simple, powerful form of UQ for neural networks.
    
- **Bayesian Neural Networks & Dropout:** Another approach is to use **Monte Carlo dropout** or other Bayesian approximations at inference to get uncertainty. By enabling dropout during prediction and sampling the network output multiple times, one can obtain a distribution of predictions without training multiple models. This technique, while less explored in published SAPT ML work, could be applied to AP-Net or an equivariant transformer to get uncertainty estimates per component. If, say, the predicted Edisp has a high uncertainty while other components are stable, that might indicate an unusual dispersion interaction not seen in training. In general, one would look at the combined uncertainty of the total interaction energy or any component exceeding a threshold (like 0.3 kcal/mol) as a red flag.
    
- **Gaussian Process Regression/Kernels:** CLIFF already uses kernel ridge regression for atomic parameter models, which can inherently provide uncertainty estimates (e.g. via Gaussian process variance, since KRR is a special case of GP). A descriptor falling far from the training descriptor distribution would yield a larger prediction variance. Although CLIFF’s current implementation doesn’t explicitly report uncertainties, extending it to output, for example, the variance in predicted dispersion coefficient or charge, is feasible. Those variances could be propagated through the analytical formulas to get an uncertainty in each energy component. Because CLIFF’s components are additive, one could even identify which term (electrostatics vs dispersion, etc.) is most uncertain for a given complex and thus which aspect of the interaction might need a quantum calculation for confirmation.
    
- **Thresholding Strategy:** A concrete strategy in a multi-fidelity workflow is: **compute ML-predicted SAPT0 energies for all candidate geometries; identify any case where the model’s uncertainty > 0.3 kcal/mol (or where predicted values are in a regime known to be less reliable, e.g. a very large induction energy that might indicate a rare strong polarization case); for those cases, perform an actual SAPT0 (or higher-level) calculation to get a definitive answer.** This ensures that in regions where the model extrapolates, the risk of large error is mitigated by fallback to QM. Such an approach leverages the **speed of ML while maintaining the accuracy guarantees of ab initio** for the outliers.
    
- **Active Learning:** Uncertainty quantification also enables **active learning (AL)** – the model can request more data in regions of high uncertainty to improve itself. This has proven extremely efficient in other chemical ML contexts. For example, the ANI neural potential was built using AL to sample only the most informative new molecules, achieving the same accuracy as a random approach with only ~10% of the data. A similar philosophy can be applied to SAPT0 models: one could start with a moderate dataset (like a subset of Splinter or SAPT10K) and train a model, then iteratively identify dimers (or configurations) where the model’s predictions are most uncertain or where an ensemble disagrees, compute the true SAPT0 for those, add them to the training set, and retrain. Over a few iterations, this **query-by-committee** strategy could greatly expand the model’s reliability without requiring an exhaustive dataset from the outset. In essence, the model itself guides where new SAPT calculations are needed, focusing computational resources on the difficult cases. This is especially useful for covering edge cases in chemical space or rare interaction motifs.
    
- **Multi-fidelity Transfer Learning:** Another powerful tool is to combine **different levels of theory**. SAPT0 is a reasonably accurate but not perfect method; one might want a model ultimately predictive of higher-level results (e.g. CCSD(T)/CBS energies). AP-Net demonstrated a transfer learning approach where a model pre-trained on the large SAPT0 dataset was fine-tuned on a much smaller CCSD(T) dataset (DES370K, 370k dimers). The pre-training gave it a strong foundation, and fine-tuning adjusted the predictions to match the “gold-standard” energies. The result was striking: with only 1,000 high-level data points, the transfer-learned model attained ~0.37 kcal/mol MAE to CCSD(T), whereas a model trained from scratch on those 1,000 points was around 0.80 kcal/mol MAE. In fact, the transfer-learned model with 1k data was as accurate as a full-data (50k points) model trained from scratch. This illustrates a multi-fidelity strategy: use a fast lower-fidelity method (SAPT0) to generate lots of data and train a base model, then use a **small amount of expensive data** to calibrate the model to the higher fidelity. For users, this means one can deploy the SAPT0-trained model for most predictions and only occasionally compute a CCSD(T) point; or if ultimate accuracy is needed, fine-tune once and then use the refined model. The approach effectively **amplifies the value of each expensive data point** – each high-level calculation corrects the model for an entire neighborhood of similar systems, guided by the lower-level trends that the model already learned. This kind of transfer learning is directly applicable to multi-fidelity SAPT workflows (for instance, training on cheaper SAPT0/jun-cc-pVDZ and correcting to SAPT2+ or SAPT0/aug-cc-pVTZ levels).
    

In practice, combining these ideas yields a robust workflow: use an ensemble of a SAPT0-trained GNN to screen lots of complexes; as needed, perform higher-level calculations on flagged complexes (uncertainty above threshold or particularly critical cases); incorporate those results either immediately via transfer learning or in a retraining cycle later. Such a workflow would maintain speed while systematically improving accuracy where the model is weakest.

## Active Learning Strategies for Improvement

As mentioned, active learning (AL) can play a pivotal role in refining ML models for SAPT0. Given an initial model, one can algorithmically explore the space of dimer configurations to **find where the model is likely to be wrong**. Common strategies include:

- **Query by Committee (Ensemble AL):** Use a committee of models (e.g. 5 networks trained on slightly different data subsets or with different architectures) to evaluate a pool of unlabeled candidates. Select those candidates with the highest variance in predictions to compute with SAPT0 and add to the training set. This directly uses disagreement as a proxy for uncertainty. It was the strategy behind the ANI-1x dataset generation, where new molecules were sampled from an MD simulation and only those with high committee disagreement were selected for expensive DFT calculations. Transferring this to intermolecular problems, one could, for example, randomly generate dimer orientations or use molecular dynamics to sample configurations, then pick those where the committee is unsure (say, one model predicts a binding energy of -5 kcal/mol while another predicts -2 kcal/mol). By labeling those via SAPT0, the model’s knowledge of that region (perhaps a specific orientation or functional group interaction) becomes much more accurate.
    
- **Uncertainty thresholding (Single-model AL):** Even with a single model, one can use internal measures like prediction variance (via dropout) or the distance of the input to training examples (in descriptor space for CLIFF or latent space for a NN) to gauge uncertainty. If the model produces a result with a confidence below a certain level, that input is a candidate for high-fidelity computation and retraining. For instance, CLIFF could examine if a new dimer’s atomic descriptor lies outside the convex hull of descriptors in its training set; if yes, it’s a novel case and computing its SAPT components would be valuable to avoid extrapolation errors.
    
- **Exploring High-Uncertainty Regions:** Some interactions are inherently more complex (e.g. strong induction between highly polar molecules, or dispersion between very large aromatic systems). An AL approach can deliberately **target the extremes** of parameter space – e.g. very large dipole moments, very close contact distances – to ensure the model learns the correct behavior there. This was partly done in constructing Splinter: the dataset includes many random orientations and distances (50–500 configurations per fragment pair), which is a brute-force way to cover configuration space. AL could make this more efficient by focusing on those geometries where a preliminary model’s predicted forces or energies change rapidly (indicating uncertainty in the shape of the PES). By augmenting the training set in such high-uncertainty regions, one can **reduce the model’s maximum error** significantly, not just the average error.
    
- **Adaptive Sampling in Multi-fidelity Context:** One could start by sampling with a cheaper method (say, a quick DFT or even force field) to find a wide array of dimers, then use the ML model to predict SAPT0 for all, and identify any outliers for actual SAPT0 computation. This multi-step AL ensures diversity (from the cheap initial sampling) and focuses SAPT0 computations where the ML model (informed by cheaper data) is least certain. Over time, this builds a multi-fidelity dataset where most points have only an approximate label but key points have exact labels, and the model is trained to interpolate between exact points – a kind of piecewise correction approach.
    

Active learning in the noncovalent domain is still an emerging area, but given the success in other domains, it holds a lot of promise for **minimizing the number of expensive QM calculations needed** to train highly accurate SAPT predictors. A conservative estimate from analogous studies is that only ~10–20% of a randomly selected dataset might be needed if AL is used intelligently to choose samples. For a dataset like Splinter, that could mean achieving the same accuracy with perhaps tens of thousands instead of over a million SAPT0 calculations – a huge saving in computation if one were building a dataset from scratch.

## Conclusions and Outlook

In summary, recent advances show that it is feasible to predict SAPT0 energy components from 3D molecular geometries with high fidelity using machine learning. **Graph neural networks and SE(3)-equivariant networks** stand out for their ability to directly process structures and learn complex intra- and inter-molecular interactions without human-crafted features. Models like **AP-Net** have demonstrated excellent accuracy (on the order of a few tenths of a kcal/mol) for all SAPT0 components across diverse test cases, and do so at speeds that enable **interactive screening** (seconds per prediction vs. hours for ab initio). These networks benefit from large training datasets and incorporate physical insights (like long-range electrostatics) to generalize well. On the other hand, **descriptor-based models** like **CLIFF** leverage known physical decompositions to achieve comparable accuracy with far fewer training examples, and offer interpretability by design (each energy component is explicitly computed from a known functional form) ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=interaction%20energies%20with%20machine,side)) ([CLIFF: A component-based, machine-learned, intermolecular force field - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34241025/#:~:text=electrostatic%2C%20exchange,protein%20fragments%2C%20the%20CLIFF%20produces)). CLIFF’s efficiency and integration into Python pipelines make it a practical choice when its domain of applicability (common bio-organic functional groups) overlaps with the problem at hand.

When comparing models, one should consider the use-case: if one expects to encounter very novel chemistries or needs to scale to millions of predictions, a deep learning model (with possible active learning assistance) might be preferable for its flexibility and scalability. If one’s focus is on _known types of interactions_ (e.g. typical drug-like molecules, protein fragments), a physics-based ML model provides a fast and reliable solution that is less of a “black box.” In many cases, a **hybrid strategy** could yield the best results – for example, using a GNN like AP-Net for its broad coverage, but correcting it with a small number of high-level calculations or a secondary model for any systematic deviations (as in the transfer learning to CCSD(T) approach).

For **multi-fidelity SAPT workflows**, the outlook is very promising. One can envision a tiered setup: initial screening with a fast SAPT0-ML (like AP-Net or CLIFF) to evaluate thousands of candidate complexes; identification of the top candidates or any uncertain predictions; refinement of those using higher-level methods or an improved ML model fine-tuned via transfer learning. Throughout this process, uncertainty quantification is the guiding light ensuring that the ML model is used within its competence regime and augmented when it steps beyond. The combination of **fast GNN predictions**, **uncertainty-guided refinement**, and **active learning** forms a virtuous cycle: as the workflow operates, the ML model becomes more robust and the need for expensive calculations drops further.

Finally, the availability of **open-source implementations** accelerates progress and adoption. Researchers can experiment with AP-Net (TensorFlow) or TorchMD-NET (PyTorch) easily, or plug CLIFF’s Python module into existing simulation code to obtain SAPT0-like component insights. These tools make it simpler to integrate SAPT decompositions into large-scale modeling – for instance, one could incorporate a SAPT0-ML potential into molecular dynamics to capture directional induction and dispersion effects in a protein binding site on-the-fly, something impractical with direct quantum calculations. As these models continue to improve in accuracy and as training datasets grow (e.g. the recent **SAPT20k or SAPT/AI datasets** expanding the diversity of dimers), we expect **multi-fidelity workflows** to become standard in computational chemistry. They will allow users to **seamlessly traverse between fast approximations and rigorous quantum methods**, with ML providing the bridge that ensures speed without sacrificing the detailed insight that SAPT offers. This synergy of physics and machine learning is directly enabling new levels of efficiency in predicting noncovalent interactions, which will benefit drug design, materials engineering, and our general understanding of intermolecular forces.
